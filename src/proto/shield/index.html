Shield Up

Step 1e1. The kart piece with something chasing it. Drive through an item box to pick up a banana; when the siren says a red shell has locked on from behind, hold the item key and the banana trails behind your bumper and takes the hit. Chapter 2's whole lesson is that holding it costs you nothing, so hold it.

Drive

← → or A/D to steer. Shift holds the banana behind you. R resets.

There is no magic shield here, just a banana. Hold Shift and it sits behind your bumper where the shell will hit it instead of you. Let go and it does what a banana does: it lands on the road and stays there while you drive away. It can still catch the shell from down there — it is in the road, after all — but only if the shell happens to come through where you left it. Held, it cannot miss.

Bananas come from the item boxes, four rows of them spread around the lap. Blocking a shell uses yours up, so after a good block the next job is finding another box.

Roughly a third of the warnings are fake: identical sirens, identical approach, and then the thing veers off in the last fraction of a second. They are not there to punish holding. They are there to bait the flinch — the relieved release that leaves your banana on the tarmac with the next siren already starting.

Windows note: holding right Shift for eight seconds pops up the Filter Keys prompt. Use the left one, or rebind the item key on the input prototype. Worth knowing before we pick final bindings at 1g1.

    What to feel for

    Rehearsal for gate 1e2, which asks two things. Is the threat legible? There is deliberately no top-down view on this page: an instrument showing the shell closing in from above would answer the question for you. The siren, the draining bar and the pips are all you get, which is all Jodi will get.

    Is the hold-the-banana habit forming after two minutes? The honest test is whether you start holding before the siren rather than after it. If you are still reacting at the end of two minutes, the warning lead is probably too short — move it on the panel and say what felt right.

    Also worth a verdict: the fake-outs. Thirty per cent is a guess. Too many and the sirens stop meaning anything; too few and the relieved release never gets caught out. And the sound — if the pips are annoying rather than useful, that is a real finding, not a nitpick.

    One number I cannot judge without playing it: banana catch radius, on the config panel above. It decides whether dropping the banana is a fair last-second defence or a gamble. Too generous and letting go becomes as good as holding, which teaches the exact opposite of Chapter 2. Watch how often drop-blocked shows up in the log.
